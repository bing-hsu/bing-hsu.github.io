## Algebraic Formation

> Introduced by statistician David Cox in 1972

The Cox PH model specifies the **hazard rate** at lifetime $t$ for an individual
with covariate vector $X = (X_1, X_2, \dots, X_p)$ as:

$$h(t \mid X) = h_0(t) \exp(\beta^T X)= h_0(t) \exp\left(\sum_{j=1}^p \beta_j X_j\right)$$

- **$h(t \mid X)$ (Hazard Function)**

$$h(t) = \lim_{\Delta t \to 0} \frac{P(t \le T < t + \Delta t \mid T \ge t)}{\Delta t}$$

- **$h_0(t)$ (Baseline Hazard):** The hazard function for an individual whose
  covariates are all zero ($X = 0$).

  > $h_0(t)$ captures the dynamics of risk purely as a function of time.

- **$\exp(\beta^T X)$ (Relative Hazard Effect):** A multiplicative factor that
  scales the baseline hazard up or down based on the individual's covariates.

### The Proportional Hazards (PH) Assumption

Using the Cox PH Model, if we compare two covariate profiles, $A$ and $B$, their
ratio of hazards is a constant:

$$
\frac{h(t \mid X_A)}{h(t \mid X_B)} = \frac{h_0(t) \exp(\beta^T X_A)}{h_0(t) \exp(\beta^T X_B)}
= \frac{\exp(\beta^T X_A)}{\exp(\beta^T X_B)} = \exp\left(\beta^T (X_A - X_B)\right)
$$

where $X_A, X_B$ are both observed and fixed; $\beta$ is estimated, but also
fixed after model is fit to data.

> Cox PH assumes that differences between **customer profiles** correspond to
> stable hazard ratios over time. If profile A has a **20% lower hazard than
> profile B** at $t_0$, it also has a 20% lower hazard at $t_{100}$:
>
> $$\frac{h(t \mid A)}{h(t \mid B)} = 0.8$$
>
> However, it does not require $h(t_0 \mid A) = h(t_{100} \mid A)$. Hazards can
> rise, fall, or follow a hump-shaped trajectory—as long as they remain
> proportional.

### Interpreting Coefficients: Hazard Ratio (HR)

For **$p$-dimensional covariates**:

$$X_A, X_B, \beta \in \mathbb{R}^p$$

We call the term $\frac{h(t \mid X_A)}{h(t \mid X_B)}$ the **Hazard Ratio**
between $A$ and $B$ - $\operatorname{HR}_{A:B}$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\operatorname{HR}_{A:B} = \frac{h(t \mid X_A)}{h(t \mid X_B)} = \frac{h_0(t) \exp(\beta^T X_A)}{h_0(t) \exp(\beta^T X_B)}
= \frac{\exp(\beta^T X_A)}{\exp(\beta^T X_B)} = \exp\left(\beta^T (X_A - X_B)\right)
\end{aligned}
$$

The sign of the term $\beta^\top (X_A - X_B)$ **indicates** the relative hazard
effect direction:

- **$= 0$:** HR $= 1$; equal hazards.
- **$> 0$:** HR $> 1$; $A$ assumes higher hazard.
- **$< 0$:** HR $< 1$; $A$ assumes lower hazard.

Quantitatively, an individual coefficient $\beta_j$ is the change in log hazard
associated with a one-unit increase in covariate $X_j$**.**

If A and B differ only by one unit in $X_j$:

$$\operatorname{HR}_{A:B} = e^{\beta_j}$$

- **$\beta_j = 0$:** no **difference in hazard**; implies a change in $X_j$ does
  not affect hazard (a potentially irrelevant feature).
- **$\beta_j = 0.2 > 0$:** (**Harmful Factor $X_j$**) one additional unit of
  $X_j$ corresponds to $e^{0.2} \approx 1.22$ times the hazard—about **22%
  higher**.
- **$\beta_j = -0.2 < 0$:** (**Protective Factor $X_j$**) one additional unit
  corresponds to $e^{-0.2} \approx 0.82$ times the hazard—about **18% lower**.

## Connection to the Survival Function

> CDF (Failure Function), CCDF (Survival Function), PDF, Hazard: these are all
> related concepts; knowing one, you can derive the others.

The survival function $S(t) = P(T > t)$ can be represented using the cumulative
hazard $H(t) = \int_0^t h(u) \, du$:

$$S(t \mid X) = \exp\left(-H(t \mid X)\right) = \exp\left(-H_0(t) \exp(\beta^T X)\right) = \left[ S_0(t) \right]^{\exp(\beta^T X)}$$

where $S_0(t) = \exp(-H_0(t))$ is the baseline survival function.

## Estimation: Partial Likelihood

Because $h_0(t)$ is unspecified, standard Maximum Likelihood Estimation cannot
be applied directly. Cox solved this by constructing a **Partial Likelihood**
that isolates the parameters $\beta$ from $h_0(t)$.

1. Rank the unique observed event times in ascending order:
   $t_1 < t_2 < \dots < t_k$.
2. At each event time $t_i$, identify the **risk set** $R(t_i)$.
3. Condition on the fact that an event occurred at time $t_i$. The probability
   that it happened to subject $i$ specifically is:

$$L_i(\beta) = \frac{h(t_i \mid X_i)}{\sum_{j \in R(t_i)} h(t_i \mid X_j)} = \frac{h_0(t_i) \exp(\beta^T X_i)}{\sum_{j \in R(t_i)} h_0(t_i) \exp(\beta^T X_j)} = \frac{\exp(\beta^T X_i)}{\sum_{j \in R(t_i)} \exp(\beta^T X_j)}$$

4. The baseline hazard $h_0(t_i)$ cancels out.
5. Multiply these probabilities over all $k$ event times to form the overall
   partial likelihood:

$$L(\beta) = \prod_{i=1}^k \frac{\exp(\beta^T X_i)}{\sum_{j \in R(t_i)} \exp(\beta^T X_j)}$$

Maximizing $\ln L(\beta)$ yields the coefficient estimates $\hat{\beta}$ and
their variance-covariance matrix.

### A Concrete Example

Using the same CRM snapshot, add the covariate **premium support**: $X = 1$ if
present, $X = 0$ otherwise. The support assignments below are illustrative and
assumed fixed throughout follow-up.

| Customer_ID | signup_date | churn_date | status   | Query Date | Tenure $T$ (months) | Event $\delta$ | Premium support $X$ |
| ----------- | ----------- | ---------- | -------- | ---------- | ------------------: | -------------: | ------------------: |
| **User_A**  | 2025-11-01  | 2026-08-01 | Canceled | 2026-10-01 |                   9 |              1 |                   1 |
| **User_B**  | 2025-08-01  | NULL       | Active   | 2026-10-01 |                  14 |              0 |                   1 |
| **User_C**  | 2024-12-01  | 2026-09-01 | Canceled | 2026-10-01 |                  21 |              1 |                   0 |
| **User_D**  | 2024-04-01  | NULL       | Active   | 2026-10-01 |                  30 |              0 |                   1 |

Because Cox PH structures $h(t \mid X) = h_0(t) \exp(\beta^T X)$ and we have a
1-dim binary covariate, the linear predictor simplifies to $\beta$:

$$h(t \mid X) = h_0(t) \cdot \exp(\beta)$$

The likelihood of an observation $i$:

$$L_i =  \frac{\exp(\beta)}{\sum_{j \in R} \exp(\beta)}$$

Writing $q = \exp(\beta)$ (**with** $q = 1$ when $X=0$)**:**

$$L_i =  \frac{q}{\sum_{j \in R} q}$$

| Tenure        | Observation | Risk Set   | Relative hazard scores | Partial-likelihood contribution |
| ------------- | ----------- | ---------- | ---------------------- | ------------------------------- |
| **9 months**  | A churns    | A, B, C, D | $q,\ q,\ 1,\ q$        | $\displaystyle \frac{q}{3q+1}$  |
| **21 months** | C churns    | C, D       | $1,\ q$                | $\displaystyle \frac{1}{1+q}$   |

Multiplying the two event contributions gives:

$$L(q) = \frac{q}{3q+1}\cdot\frac{1}{1+q}$$

With this simple form, we can solve for the $\beta$ that maximizes the
likelihood analytically:

First, expand the denominator and differentiate and solve for the derivative
that is equal to zero:

$$L'(q) = \frac{1 - 3q^2}{(3q^2 + 4q + 1)^2} = 0$$

$$1 - 3q^2 = 0 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \hat{q} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{3}}$$

This is the **global maximum**.

Transforming back to $\beta$:

$$\hat{\beta} = \ln\hat{q} = -\frac{1}{2}\ln 3 \approx -0.5493$$

The fitted hazard ratio:

$$\widehat{\operatorname{HR}} = e^{\hat{\beta}} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{3}} \approx 0.5774$$

The fitted model associates premium support with approximately **42.3% lower
hazard**.

## Diagnostics and Handling Violations

PH is violated when:

$$\frac{h(t \mid X_A)}{h(t \mid X_B)} \quad \text{ is not a constant}$$

This takes place **when the effect difference between profile A and B varies at
different times.** In other words, their relative effect depends on time.

change from this:

$$\frac{h_A(t)}{h_B(t)} = c \qquad \text{constant proportionality}$$

to become this:

$$\frac{h_A(t)}{h_B(t)} = c(t) \qquad \text{changing proportionality}$$

For example, suppose A receives an intervention and B does not:

- **A benefit wears off.** A welcome discount initially discourages churn, but
  its advantage disappears after expiration. HR might rise from $0.5$ toward
  $1$.
- **A benefit takes time to develop.** An onboarding program initially makes
  little difference, but customers gradually learn to use the product. HR might
  fall from $1$ toward $0.6$.
- **The environment changes the advantage.** A lower-priced plan may offer
  little retention advantage during prosperous periods but a stronger advantage
  during a recession.
- **The advantage reverses.** A complex product initially frustrates customers,
  but becomes valuable after they master it. HR might start above $1$ and later
  fall below $1$.

### Diagnostic Methods

- **Schoenfeld Residuals:** At each event time $t_i$, the Schoenfeld residual is
  the difference between the covariate value of the failing individual and the
  risk-weighted average of that covariate across the risk set $R(t_i)$. If
  **the** proportional hazards **assumption** holds, a plot of these residuals
  against time shows a flat trend around zero. A non-zero slope (often evaluated
  using Grambsch-Therneau tests) signals a time-dependent effect.
- **Log-Log Survival Plots:** Plotting $\ln(-\ln(S(t)))$ versus $\ln(t)$ for
  discrete groups should yield parallel curves. Non-parallel or crossing curves
  indicate non-proportional hazards.

### Remedies for Non-Proportionality

- **Stratified Cox Model:** If a covariate violates the assumption but its
  specific regression coefficient is not of interest (e.g., study center or
  biological sex), stratify by that variable. This allows each stratum to have
  its own baseline hazard $h_{0g}(t)$ while preserving common coefficients
  $\beta$ for the remaining variables.
- **Time-Varying Coefficients:** Model the effect as an explicit function of
  time, $\beta(t) = \beta_0 + \beta_1 \ln(t)$, using an interaction term between
  the covariate and time.
- **Parametric Alternatives:** When hazard ratios vary over time, an Accelerated
  Failure Time (AFT) model or Restricted Mean Survival Time (RMST) analysis can
  serve as more appropriate frameworks.
