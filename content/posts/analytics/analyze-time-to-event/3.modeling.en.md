## Before Modeling, Check Out the Kaplan-Meier (KM) Curve

{{< series-link "posts/analytics/analyze-time-to-event/3.1.km-curve.en.md" "Read the detailed guide: Section 3.1 — Kaplan-Meier (KM) Curve →" >}}

Before choosing a model, inspect the survival pattern already visible in the
sample. The **Kaplan–Meier (KM) estimator** builds a non-parametric estimate of
the survival function directly from observed event times and censoring
indicators:

$$\hat{S}(t) = \widehat{P}(T^* > t)$$

> A KM curve plays a role similar to a scatter plot before regression: it lets
> you inspect the observed pattern before deciding how to model it.

Use this curve as an empirical starting point, then turn to Cox PH or AFT when
you need to connect survival outcomes to covariates.

## Methods to Study: Cox PH and AFT

- {{< series-link "posts/analytics/analyze-time-to-event/3.2.cox-ph.en.md" "Section 3.2 — Cox Proportional Hazards (Cox PH) Model →" >}}
- {{< series-link "posts/analytics/analyze-time-to-event/3.3.weibull-aft.en.md" "Section 3.3 — Weibull AFT →" >}}

**Cox PH first**, **Weibull AFT second**

Together, they form the foundational curriculum of survival analysis and cover
the vast majority of real-world use cases.

- **Cox PH** operates on the hazard rate ($h(t \mid x)$). It answers: _"At any
  given moment, how much more likely is group A to experience the event compared
  to group B?"_
- **AFT** operates on time ($T$ or $\ln T$). It answers: _"How much longer or
  shorter is the expected lifespan for group A compared to group B?"_

**Each Other's Backup**

Cox PH relies on the **proportional hazards** assumption (the hazard ratio
remains constant over time). When this assumption fails, an AFT model often
serves as the alternative because it does not assume proportional hazards.

Conversely, if you are unsure which parametric distribution fits your data and
do not want to risk distributional misspecification, Cox PH is a safer default
than AFT.

**Industry Practice**

In biostatistics, epidemiology, and clinical trials, Cox PH is the default
standard demanded by journals and regulatory bodies (FDA, EMA).

In reliability engineering, manufacturing, and warranty analysis, AFT is the
preferred standard because stakeholders care about time-to-failure rather than
hazard ratios.

Both are mature and fully implemented in standard libraries (such as `lifelines`
in Python).

**Which AFT Model to Pick**

As AFT requires assuming a parametric distribution family, the recommended first
pick is **the Weibull AFT model.**

The Weibull distribution is the **only distribution that is simultaneously a
Proportional Hazards model and an Accelerated Failure Time model**.

Studying the Weibull distribution allows you to see the algebraic connection
between hazard ratios ($\text{HR} = e^{\beta_{\text{PH}}}$) and acceleration
factors ($\text{AF} = e^{-\beta_{\text{AFT}}}$).

**Secondary alternative (Log-Logistic)**: If your data has an initial surge in
the event rate that later tapers off (a unimodal or "hump-shaped" hazard, common
in post-surgery recovery or customer churn), the Log-Logistic AFT model is a
good choice.

### Blind Spots to Keep in Mind

- **Competing Risks**: If a subject can experience an alternative event that
  permanently precludes the primary event (e.g., dying of cardiovascular disease
  before a cancer recurrence can happen), standard Cox and AFT models will
  overestimate risk. You would eventually need cumulative incidence methods
  (like Fine-Gray).

- **High-Dimensional Non-Linear Interactions**: Both basic Cox PH and parametric
  AFT assume linear combinations of covariates ($\beta^T X$). If you have
  complex, high-dimensional tabular data with heavy interactions and no time for
  manual feature engineering, tree-based methods (like Random Survival Forests)
  or survival gradient boosting handle those relationships more naturally.
