## The Decision Model: Forecast Plus Business Assumptions

The same estimated survival distribution powers all three operational decisions;
what changes is the **business quantity extracted from it**.

For a customer with covariate profile $x$ who remains active at tenure $t_0$,
the conditional probability of surviving an additional $u$ months is:

$$R(u \mid t_0, x) = \frac{S(t_0 + u \mid x)}{S(t_0 \mid x)}$$

While the survival model supplies this conditional retention trajectory, the
business context supplies the economic parameters required to evaluate profit
and loss—such as monthly contribution margin, target return multiples,
intervention costs, and expected save rates.

The examples below apply the fitted Cox PH and Weibull AFT models from Chapter 4
to five active customer profiles from the Telco Customer Churn dataset (source
row 0, 6, 7, 10, and 11).

## Answer to Case 2.1 — Lifetime Value (LTV) and Acquisition Decisions

> “Given the expected survival curve of a cohort (market segment, region,
> channel, etc.), what is their net discounted financial contribution over their
> entire lifecycle, and how much can we afford to spend to acquire & maintain
> them?”

![Residual Lifetime Value, Maximum Affordable CAC at a required 3:1 LTV:CAC ratio, and CAC Payback Period for five active customer profiles over the next 12 months.](/img/analytics/analysis-engine/value-and-payback.png)

> Why why max affordable CAC is significantly less than rLTV (residual lifetime
> value)?
>
> Because the example requires a **3:1 LTV:CAC ratio**:
>
> $$\text{Maximum CAC meeting the target} = \frac{\text{rLTV}}{3}$$
>
> For profile 6, that is $148.83 / 3 = 49.61$.

Examining the Cox PH estimates:

- **Profile 6:** Residual Lifetime Value (rLTV) reaches **148.83**, supporting a
  **maximum affordable CAC of 49.61** with a **CAC payback period of 3.77
  months**.
- **Profile 11:** rLTV is **33.64**, supporting a **maximum affordable CAC of
  11.21** with a **CAC payback period of 3.92 months**.

Profile 6 justifies a substantially larger acquisition ceiling due to its higher
monthly margin. Notably, the comparable payback periods reflect the time
required to recover each profile's _own_ maximum allowable CAC rather than an
identical dollar outlay.

### How a Manager Uses This Output

A manager uses these metrics to replace uniform acquisition budgets with
**segment-specific spending caps** (`Maximum Affordable CAC`). By benchmarking
actual channel acquisition costs against each cohort's allowable ceiling—and
verifying that the `CAC Payback Period` aligns with cash-flow targets—leadership
can aggressively scale spend on high-margin segments like Profile 6 while
preventing value-destroying overspend on lower-margin profiles like Profile 11.

A manager uses these lifecycle phase bands to **time proactive retention
playbooks ahead of peak churn windows** rather than reacting after cancellation
requests arrive. Specifically, customer success teams can front-load high-touch
onboarding and technical check-ins during Month 1 for month-to-month fiber-optic
subscribers (`danger II`), while keeping inherently stable one- and two-year
contract cohorts (`safe`) on low-cost automated lifecycle tracks.

## Answer to Case 2.3 — Retention Resources Allocation

> _"Given limited intervention resources over an upcoming period, which users
> should be targeted for retention intervention?"_

The right panel plots net expected intervention value,
$\mathbb{E}[\text{revenue saved}_i] - C_i$. The vertical zero line marks the
break-even threshold where expected saved revenue equals intervention cost;
profiles to the right generate a positive net return.

![Short-term churn probability and Expected Revenue Saved minus Intervention Cost for five subscribers, using the Value-at-Risk, MRR, and intervention-success notation from Case 2.3.](/img/analytics/analysis-engine/intervention-risk-and-benefit.png)

Evaluating the Cox PH results:

- **Profile 7 carries the highest short-term churn probability** at **9.45%**,
  with an **MRR of 29.75**, yielding a net expected gain
  ($\mathbb{E}[\text{revenue saved}_i] - C_i$) of **3.97**.
- **Profile 6 exhibits a lower short-term churn probability** (**4.58%**) but a
  substantially higher **MRR of 89.10**. Over a five-month horizon, its revenue
  exposure is **445.50**, producing a **Value-at-Risk (VaR) of 20.42**. At an
  assumed **55.2%** intervention success rate, expected saved revenue is
  **11.28**; subtracting the **5.56** intervention cost leaves a net gain of
  **5.72**.
- **Profile 11 poses negligible short-term churn risk** (**0.12%**), generating
  only **0.05** in expected saved revenue against a **2.66** intervention
  cost—resulting in a net loss of **−2.61** (below the zero break-even line).

### How a Manager Uses This Output

A manager uses this net-return ranking to **allocate finite retention budgets by
expected ROI rather than raw churn risk alone**. When outreach capacity is
constrained, high-MRR accounts like Profile 6 take priority over riskier but
lower-value accounts like Profile 7 because they yield greater net revenue saved
per intervention, while negative-net-value accounts like Profile 11 are
automatically filtered out of costly human outreach.
