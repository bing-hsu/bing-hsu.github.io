**Coefficients**

- **`coef` ($\hat{\beta}$):** The estimated log-hazard regression coefficient.
- **`coef lower 95%` & `coef upper 95%`:** The lower and upper bounds of the 95%
  confidence interval for $\hat{\beta}$, governed by the significance threshold
  specified during fitter initialization (`alpha=0.05`).

**Derived Hazard Metrics**

- **`HR / exp(coef)` (Hazard Ratio):** The exponentiated coefficient,
  quantifying relative instantaneous risk:
  - $\text{HR} = 1.0$: No relative effect.
  - $\text{HR} > 1.0$: Increased hazard (harmful factor).
  - $\text{HR} < 1.0$: Reduced hazard (protective factor).
- **`HR lower 95%` & `HR upper 95%`:** The 95% confidence interval for the
  hazard ratio.

**Test Statistics**

- **`se(coef)` (Standard Error):** The estimated standard deviation of
  $\hat{\beta}$, quantifying estimation uncertainty (smaller values indicate
  higher precision).
- **`cmp to` (Null Reference):** The reference value $\beta_0$ tested under the
  null hypothesis ($H_0: \beta = \beta_0$).
- **`z` (Wald Test Statistic):** The number of standard errors by which
  $\hat{\beta}$ deviates from the null reference, asymptotically following a
  standard normal distribution ($z \sim \mathcal{N}(0, 1)$):

$$z = \frac{\hat{\beta} - \text{cmp to}}{\text{se}(\hat{\beta})} \xrightarrow{d} \mathcal{N}(0, 1)$$

- **`p` (Two-Sided $p$-Value):** The probability of observing a Wald statistic
  at least as extreme as $|z|$ under the null hypothesis
  ($\beta = \text{cmp to}$).
  - **`-log2(p)` (Shannon Information /
    [S-value](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S2590113322000153)):**
    Converts $p$-values into bits of surprise calibrated against fair coin
    tosses—an S-value of $k$ indicates evidence as surprising as $k$ consecutive
    heads in $k$ tosses.
    > Expressing evidence in bits prevents cognitive distortion when
    > interpreting minuscule scientific-notation $p$-values near zero.
    - An S-value of `22.35` ($p < 0.005$; equivalent to 22.35 consecutive heads
      in 23 tosses) provides strong evidence against $H_0$.
    - An S-value of `0.88` ($p = 0.54$; equivalent to 0.88 consecutive heads in
      1 toss) indicates that the data are highly compatible with $H_0$.

## Evaluation

### Proportional Hazards (PH) Assumption Diagnostics

The diagnostic evaluation flags several covariate categories whose hazard
effects vary over time, violating the proportional hazards assumption:

<figure style="text-align: center; width: 100%; margin: 30px auto;">
<img src="/img/analytics/cox-ph-evaluation/ph-diagnostics.png" alt="Training proportional-hazards diagnostics: test statistics, raw p-values, and BH-adjusted p-values for flagged covariates" loading="lazy" />
</figure>

Consider `Contract_One year`:

While the fitted Cox model estimates that a **one-year contract** reduces
overall churn hazard by **80.9%** ($\text{HR} = 0.191$) relative to a
**month-to-month** contract (see [Protective Factors](#protective-factors)), it
violates the PH assumption ($p_{\text{BH}} = 0.000001$).

Intuitively, this non-proportionality stems from **contract lock-in and renewal
cliffs**: the protective effect of an annual contract is not uniform over time,
but fluctuates sharply across a customer's billing cycle:

- **Months 1 to 11 (Contract Lock-In):** Annual subscribers face
  early-termination penalties or prepaid commitments, suppressing churn to near
  zero while month-to-month subscribers remain free to exit at any time.
  Throughout this window, the one-year contract acts as a near-absolute barrier
  ($\text{HR} \approx 0$).
- **Month 12 (The Renewal Cliff):** Once the initial term expires at Month 12,
  dissatisfied annual customers reach a penalty-free exit window, causing their
  churn hazard to spike. Consequently, the protective gap narrows sharply at
  renewal ($\text{HR}$ jumps toward or above $1.0$).

**Interpreting $\text{HR} = 0.191$ Under Non-Proportionality**

Because the true hazard ratio oscillates between near-zero mid-contract and
elevated risk at renewal, the single estimate of **0.191** represents a
**time-averaged hazard ratio** across the observation window. It tells that that
one-year subscribers churn substantially less _overall_, even though their
attrition risk concentrates at renewal milestones.

**Formal PH Assumption Framework**

Under the Proportional Hazards assumption, for any pair of categories $\{A, B\}$
from covariate $X^i$, the hazard ratio must remain constant over time:

$$\frac{h(t \mid X^i_A)}{h(t \mid X^i_B)} = \text{constant}$$

Equivalently, in the Cox model formulation

$$h(t \mid X) = h_0(t) \exp(\beta^T X)$$

the regression vector $\beta$ is assumed to be time-invariant rather than a
function of time $\beta(t)$.

Modeling a covariate's log-hazard effect as a time-varying function

$$\beta_k(t) = \beta_k + \theta_k g(t)$$

allows us to express the PH assumption as the null hypothesis
$H_0: \theta_k = 0$.

The **Grambsch–Therneau score test** evaluates $H_0$ by testing whether the
**[Schoenfeld residuals](https://timeseriesreasoning.com/contents/schoenfeld-residuals/)**
for each covariate exhibit a systematic correlation with a specified time
transformation $g(t)$.

**False Discovery Rate (FDR) Control**

When testing multiple covariates simultaneously at a fixed nominal significance
level $\alpha$, the family-wise probability of incurring at least one false
positive grows rapidly with the number of tests.

Testing over 20 covariates in this study at $\alpha = 0.05$, the cumulative
probability of at least one false alarm reaches
$1 - (0.95)^{20} \approx 64.2\%$. Without correction, a covariate that genuinely
satisfies the PH assumption is frequently misclassified as a violation.

To address multiplicity, we apply the **Benjamini–Hochberg (BH) procedure**
(1995), which controls the False Discovery Rate (FDR)—the expected proportion of
false positives among all rejected null hypotheses—yielding **BH-adjusted
$p$-values ($p_{\text{BH}}$)**.

### Out-of-Sample Discrimination Capability

> Can the model reliably rank subjects by relative risk, despite **inaccuracy
> in** the quantitative risk measures\*_?_

On the held-out test set, the fitted model achieves a Harrell's $C$-index of
**$0.8554$**, demonstrating **strong discriminatory power**.

**Harrell’s Concordance Index ($C$-index)**

Introduced by Frank Harrell in 1982, the $C$-index extends the **Area Under the
ROC Curve (AUC)** to right-censored survival data. It measures the probability
that, for a randomly selected pair of comparable subjects, the individual who
experiences the event earlier was assigned a higher predicted risk score:

- **$C = 0.50$ (Random Guessing):** No discriminatory ability; equivalent to a
  coin toss.
- **$C = 0.60\text{–}0.70$ (Moderate):** Typical for observational clinical and
  biological risk models.
- **$C = 0.70\text{–}0.80$ (Good):** Reliably separates lower- and higher-risk
  profiles across the cohort.
- **$C > 0.80$ (Strong):** High ranking accuracy, relatively uncommon in noisy
  long-term observational data.
- **$C = 1.00$ (Perfect):** Every subject who fails earlier receives a strictly
  higher risk score than every subject who survives longer.
- **$C < 0.50$ (Inverted):** Systematic reverse ranking (assigning lower risk
  scores to higher-risk subjects).

### Out-of-Sample Event Probability Prediction Error

While Harrell's $C$-index evaluates risk _ranking_, it does not verify whether a
predicted 30% churn probability corresponds to an actual 30% empirical churn
rate. To evaluate numerical probability accuracy—bridging the gap left by
rank-based metrics—we evaluate the model using the **Brier Score**.

**Model Evaluation Results:**

- **IPCW Integrated Brier Score (Months 1–36):** `0.09042`
- **Training-KM Benchmark IBS:** `0.15210`
- **Relative Brier Improvement:** `40.6%`

Across the 36-month horizon, the model achieves an IPCW Integrated Brier Score
of `0.09042`, reflecting **strong overall probability accuracy**. Compared to
the Kaplan–Meier baseline (`0.15210`), the model reduces squared prediction
error by `40.6%`.

**Understanding the Brier Score**

Introduced by meteorologist Glenn Brier in 1950 to verify probabilistic weather
forecasts, the **Brier score** is the Mean Squared Error (MSE) of predicted
probabilities for binary outcomes.

For $N$ subjects with binary outcomes $y_i \in \{0, 1\}$ and predicted event
probabilities $\hat{p}_i \in [0, 1]$:

$$BS = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^N (y_i - \hat{p}_i)^2$$

For example, if a model predicts $\hat{p}_i = 0.80$:

- When the event occurs ($y_i = 1$), the squared error is $(1 - 0.80)^2 = 0.04$.
- When the event does not occur ($y_i = 0$), the squared error is
  $(0 - 0.80)^2 = 0.64$.

**Interpreting Brier Score Magnitudes (Lower Is Better):**

- **$0.00$ (Perfect):** Assigns $\hat{p} = 1.0$ to every event and
  $\hat{p} = 0.0$ to every non-event.
- **$< 0.10$ (Strong):** Reflects high accuracy in both risk ranking and
  probability calibration.
- **$0.10\text{–}0.20$ (Good / Useful):** Standard performance range for
  well-calibrated churn and clinical survival models.
- **$0.25$ (Uninformative Coin Toss):** Achieved by a trivial model predicting
  $\hat{p} = 0.50$ for every subject.
- **$1.00$ (Maximally Wrong):** Assigns deterministic certainty in the wrong
  direction every time ($\hat{p} = 1.0$ when $y = 0$, or $\hat{p} = 0.0$ when
  $y = 1$).

**Benchmarking Against the Historical Base Rate**

If an event occurs in $10\%$ of a cohort ($p_{\text{base}} = 0.10$), a naive
model predicting $0.10$ for every individual automatically achieves a BS score:

$$0.10 \times (1 - 0.10) = 0.09$$

Consequently, **a model must beat this baseline (a BS score lower than baseline)
to demonstrate meaningful predictive value**.

**Bier Score Variants**

- **IPCW Brier Score (Inverse Probability of Censoring Weighting):**
  > Accounts for right-censoring by weighting uncensored observations inversely
  > to their probability of remaining uncensored, allowing observed subjects to
  > represent censored peers who exited follow-up early.
- **IBS (Integrated Brier Score):**
  > Summarizes time-dependent prediction error across an entire follow-up window
  > $[t_{\min}, t_{\max}]$ by integrating the point-in-time Brier score curve
  > $BS(t)$:
  >
  > $$\text{IBS} = \int_{t_{\min}}^{t_{\max}} BS(t) \, dW(t)$$

across predicted risk strata to reveal _where_ the model is well-calibrated or
systematically biased.

#### Constructing Risk Tiers

A **risk tier** (or risk quantile/bin) groups subjects with similar predicted
event probabilities at a chosen horizon. Because individual outcomes are binary
(or censored), continuous individual risk predictions (e.g., 18.4%) cannot be
directly compared with single-subject outcomes; binning aggregates subjects so
mean predicted risk can be evaluated against observed **group event
frequencies**.

**Step 1: Predict 12-Month Churn Risk on the Training Set**

Suppose the fitted Cox model generates 12-month churn probabilities,
$\hat{P}(T \le 12 \mid X)$, for 10 representative training subjects:

| Training Subject | Predicted 12-Month Churn Risk |
| ---------------- | ----------------------------- |
| Customer 1       | $0.03$ (3%)                   |
| Customer 2       | $0.07$ (7%)                   |
| Customer 3       | $0.11$ (11%)                  |
| Customer 4       | $0.18$ (18%)                  |
| Customer 5       | $0.24$ (24%)                  |
| Customer 6       | $0.31$ (31%)                  |
| Customer 7       | $0.44$ (44%)                  |
| Customer 8       | $0.55$ (55%)                  |
| Customer 9       | $0.67$ (67%)                  |
| Customer 10      | $0.88$ (88%)                  |

**Step 2: Define Quintile Cutoffs (20% Bins)**

Partitioning the training predictions into 5 equal-sized quintiles establishes
fixed probability thresholds:

| Risk Tier                    | Percentile Coverage   | Cutoff Interval |
| ---------------------------- | --------------------- | --------------- |
| **Group 1 (Very Low Risk)**  | Lowest 20% (0th–20th) | $[0.00, 0.09]$  |
| **Group 2 (Low Risk)**       | 20th–40th             | $(0.09, 0.21]$  |
| **Group 3 (Moderate Risk)**  | 40th–60th             | $(0.21, 0.37]$  |
| **Group 4 (High Risk)**      | 60th–80th             | $(0.37, 0.61]$  |
| **Group 5 (Very High Risk)** | Top 20% (80th–100th)  | $(0.61, 1.00]$  |

**Step 3: Assign Test-Set Subjects to Training-Defined Tiers**

Applying these training thresholds to new test-set subjects assigns each
customer to a tier:

| Test Customer   | Model Predicted Churn ($\hat{p}$) | Assigned Tier |
| --------------- | --------------------------------- | ------------- |
| Test Customer A | $0.04$ (4%)                       | **Group 1**   |
| Test Customer B | $0.06$ (6%)                       | **Group 1**   |
| Test Customer C | $0.15$ (15%)                      | **Group 2**   |
| Test Customer D | $0.28$ (28%)                      | **Group 3**   |
| Test Customer E | $0.50$ (50%)                      | **Group 4**   |
| Test Customer F | $0.72$ (72%)                      | **Group 5**   |

**Step 4: Compute Plotted Coordinates**

Using **Group 1** in the test set as an example:

- **Horizontal Coordinate ($X$-axis — Mean Predicted Churn):**
  $$\frac{0.04 + 0.06}{2} = 0.05 \quad (5\%)$$
- **Vertical Coordinate ($Y$-axis — Observed Test KM Churn):** Fitting a
  Kaplan–Meier curve exclusively to test subjects in Group 1 yields zero
  observed churns by Month 12:
  $$\text{KM Churn at Month 12} = 1 - \hat{S}(12) = 0.00 \quad (0\%)$$

This yields the plotted coordinate **$(X = 0.05, Y = 0.00)$**, resting near the
origin without a confidence interval bar because Greenwood's variance is zero
when no events occur.
