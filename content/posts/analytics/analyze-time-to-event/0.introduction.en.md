Most analytics questions ask **what happened** or **whether something will
happen**. Time-to-event analysis adds another question: **when?**

How long will a customer keep subscribing? When does churn risk rise? How does
that timing affect what we can spend on acquisition, or when a retention team
should act?

**Analyze Time-to-Event** explores the connection between those questions, the
mathematical tools used to investigate them, and the decisions they can support.
This page is the starting point; the series roadmap above links to every chapter
and case.

## How the series fits together

### 1 — Data Characteristics and Math Toolset

Start with the structure of time-to-event data, including censoring and latent
event times. Then build the mathematical vocabulary: survival functions, hazard,
cumulative hazard, and the modeling approaches behind Cox and AFT models.

### 2 — Business Cases

Move from estimating survival to framing business decisions. The chapter
introduces three connected perspectives:

- **2.1 — Lifetime Value and Acquisition Decisions:** connect expected survival
  to customer value, acquisition budgets, and payback constraints.
- **2.2 — Lifetime Phase Characterization:** examine risk windows across
  customer tenure and the lead time needed to prepare a response.
- **2.3 — Retention Resources Allocation:** bring near-term risk, value at
  stake, intervention effectiveness, and operational capacity into a
  prioritization framework.

{{< series-link "posts/analytics/analyze-time-to-event/2.business-cases.en.md" "Read chapter 2 →" >}}

### 3 — Modeling Choice

Move from the business question to a model of the event-time distribution.
Begin with an empirical survival curve, then study two ways to connect customer
profiles to survival outcomes:

- **3.1 — Kaplan-Meier (KM) Curve:** build a survival estimate from observed
  event times and censoring before fitting a regression model.
- **3.2 — Cox PH:** formulate proportional hazards, interpret hazard ratios,
  and understand estimation through partial likelihood.
- **3.3 — Weibull AFT:** model how covariates stretch or compress event times,
  interpret time ratios, and examine the Weibull distributional assumption.

{{< series-link "posts/analytics/analyze-time-to-event/3.modeling.en.md" "Read chapter 3 →" >}}

### 4 — Analysis Implementation

Apply the methods to the Telco Customer Churn dataset. Start with dataset
inspection and covariate preparation, then examine fitted results and the checks
needed to judge their usefulness:

- **4.1 — Kaplan-Meier (KM) Curves:** inspect unadjusted survival patterns
  across demographic, contract, payment, and service groups.
- **4.2 — Cox PH:** fit the model, interpret adjusted hazard associations, and
  evaluate proportional-hazards diagnostics and held-out predictions.
- **4.3 — Weibull AFT:** interpret adjusted time ratios, assess held-out
  discrimination, prediction error, and calibration, and compare with Cox PH.

{{< series-link "posts/analytics/analyze-time-to-event/4.analysis-impl.en.md" "Read chapter 4 →" >}}

## Choose a reading path

If the subject is new to you, follow chapters 1–4: learn the data structure,
frame the business questions, study the models, and inspect the implementation.
For a decision-focused reading, start with chapter 2. Use chapter 3 as the model
reference, or go directly to chapter 4 for the worked analysis and evaluation.

The business cases are analytical framing exercises using the TelCo Customer
Churn dataset. They develop proposed decision frameworks, rather than report
validated commercial results. Predicting churn risk and estimating the effect of
an intervention are different tasks; useful decisions require attention to both
the model and its assumptions.
