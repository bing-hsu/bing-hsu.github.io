Most analytics questions focus on **what happened** or **whether an event will
occur**. Time-to-event analysis introduces a critical third dimension: **when?**

How long will a subscriber remain active? At what point in their tenure does
churn risk escalate? How does that timing govern allowable acquisition spend, or
determine when a retention team must intervene?

**Analyze Time-to-Event** bridges these business questions, the mathematical
machinery required to model them, and the operational decisions they inform.
This page serves as the series entry point; the roadmap above links directly to
every chapter and case study.

## How the Series Fits Together

### 1 — Data Characteristics and Math Toolset

Examine the unique structure of time-to-event data—including censoring and
latent event times—and establish the core mathematical vocabulary: survival
functions, hazard rates, cumulative hazards, and the foundations of Cox and AFT
modeling.

### 2 — Business Cases

Translate survival estimates into structured commercial decisions across three
connected operational lenses:

- **2.1 — Lifetime Value and Acquisition Decisions:** Link expected survival
  trajectories to customer lifetime value, acquisition spending caps, and
  payback constraints.
- **2.2 — Lifetime Phase Characterization:** Identify high-risk tenure windows
  and the operational lead time required for proactive outreach.
- **2.3 — Retention Resources Allocation:** Integrate short-term churn risk,
  revenue at stake, intervention effectiveness, and team capacity into a unified
  prioritization framework.

{{< series-link "posts/analytics/analyze-time-to-event/2.business-cases.en.md" "Read Chapter 2 →" >}}

### 3 — Modeling Choice

Translate business questions into formal models of the event-time distribution,
progressing from non-parametric curves to covariate-driven regression:

- **3.1 — Kaplan-Meier (KM) Curve:** Estimate empirical survival directly from
  observed event times and right-censored records prior to regression modeling.
- **3.2 — Cox PH:** Formulate proportional hazards, interpret adjusted hazard
  ratios, and understand estimation via partial likelihood.
- **3.3 — Weibull AFT:** Parameterize how covariates accelerate or decelerate
  event times, interpret time ratios, and evaluate the Weibull distributional
  assumption.

{{< series-link "posts/analytics/analyze-time-to-event/3.modeling.en.md" "Read Chapter 3 →" >}}

### 4 — Analysis Implementation

Apply these methods end-to-end on the Telco Customer Churn dataset—from feature
preparation to model fitting and out-of-sample validation:

- **4.1 — Kaplan-Meier (KM) Curves:** Inspect unadjusted survival curves across
  demographic, contract, billing, and service cohorts.
- **4.2 — Cox PH:** Fit the multivariable model, interpret adjusted hazard
  associations, and run proportional-hazards diagnostics alongside held-out
  evaluations.
- **4.3 — Weibull AFT:** Interpret adjusted time ratios, evaluate out-of-sample
  discrimination, Brier error, and calibration, and benchmark performance
  against Cox PH.

{{< series-link "posts/analytics/analyze-time-to-event/4.analysis-impl.en.md" "Read Chapter 4 →" >}}

### 5 — Analysis Engine

Operationalize model outputs into actionable decision metrics: residual lifetime
value, maximum allowable CAC under a target return multiple, CAC payback period,
lifecycle risk phases, and net expected intervention return. These worked
examples demonstrate how to combine statistical forecasts with explicit business
assumptions to guide managerial judgment.

## Choose a Reading Path

If you are new to survival analysis, read **Chapters 1–5** sequentially to
master the data structure, business framing, mathematical models, implementation
details, and decision outputs. For an executive or decision-first path, start at
**Chapter 2** and jump to **Chapter 5**, consulting **Chapter 3** as a
mathematical reference and **Chapter 4** for empirical validation.

These business cases serve as analytical framing exercises built on the Telco
Customer Churn dataset; they illustrate structured decision frameworks rather
than validated commercial outcomes. Predicting baseline churn risk and
estimating causal intervention lift remain distinct problems—sound managerial
decisions depend on scrutinizing both the model outputs and their underlying
business assumptions.
