## Algebraic Formation

> **The "Countdown-Clock to Event" Metaphor**
>
> Think of the collective state of an individual as a countdown clock measuring
> the progression toward an event.
>
> Covariates act as modulators of this clock:
>
> - Deceleration (The "Prolonger"): The clock ticks more slowly. A subject
>   exposed to a protective factor takes longer to reach an event. The time axis
>   is stretched.
>   > Depending on **whether** the event is desirable: Protective (undesirable)
>   > or Delaying Factor (desirable)
> - Acceleration (The "Compactor"): The clock ticks faster. A subject exposed to
>   a risk factor reaches an event in a shorter timeframe. The time axis is
>   compressed.
>   > Harmful (undesirable) or Accelerating Factor (desirable)

### AFT (Accelerated Failure Time)

> It borrows the idea from **"accelerated life testing"**—engineers deliberately
> subject parts to extreme stress to force them to degrade faster—the covariates
> work like "stress factors" that **accelerate** the time-to-failure.
>
> What is missing from "accelerated life testing" is that AFT can also capture
> **"relaxing factors"**—those that delay the time-to-failure.

AFT's algebraic form:

$$\ln(T) = \mu +  \boldsymbol{\beta}^\top \mathbf{x}  + \sigma \epsilon$$

> In time scale:
>
> $$T = \exp(\mu + \sigma \epsilon) \cdot \exp(\mathbf{x}^T \boldsymbol{\beta}) = T_0 \cdot \exp(\mathbf{x}^T \boldsymbol{\beta})$$

Response Term:

- $\ln T$: log event-time / lifetime / failure-time
  - $T$: The random variable representing event-time / lifetime

Explainable Factors:

- $\mu$: baseline log-time.
- $\boldsymbol{\beta}^\top \mathbf{x}$: linear predictor, acts as a shift on the
  baseline log-time
  - $\mathbf{x} \in \mathbf{R}^{k \times 1}$: $k$ covariates.
  - $\boldsymbol{\beta} \in \mathbf{R}^{k \times 1}$: coefficients per
    covariate.

Error Term:

- $\sigma$: the scale parameter ($\sigma > 0$).
- $\epsilon$: the standardized error.

> Why separate $\sigma$ and $\epsilon$, see [Location-Scale Family]({{< relref "posts/analytics/general/location-scale-family.en.md" >}})

> [Matrix Notation
> (Dataset-level)]({{< relref "posts/analytics/general/dataset-level-notation.en.md" >}})
>
> With $k$ covariates / features and $n$ instances.
>
> $$\ln(\mathbf{T}) = \mathbf{1}_n \mu + \mathbf{X} \boldsymbol{\beta} + \sigma \boldsymbol{\epsilon}$$

### Why the Log Scale: $\ln T$?

The interesting part of AFT is how it models the target RV $T$ on a log scale.

**Matching Support**

Time-to-failure is strictly non-negative, $T \in [0, \infty)$, whereas $X$ is
not **necessarily** so.

If we express $T$ on the original scale:

$$T = \mu +  \boldsymbol{\beta}^\top \mathbf{x}  + \sigma \epsilon$$

the right-hand side is in $(-\infty, \infty)$.

This structure can produce negative $T$—which is impossible in reality—yet
assigns it probability mass.

> This issue is called
> [**Leakage of Probability Mass**](https://arxiv.org/html/1201.3611v1).

By modeling the response on the log scale,
$\ln T \in (-\infty, \infty) \text{ for } T \in [0, \infty)$, we match the
support to the **covariate** side:

$$\ln T = \mu +  \boldsymbol{\beta}^\top \mathbf{x}  + \sigma \epsilon$$

**Mirrors how effects work in reality**

In real-world systems, risk and protective factors tend to act
**proportionally** rather than **additively**:

> It makes more sense to say a drug "increases lifespan by 10% (relative to the
> case if you do not take it)" than to say, in absolute terms, a drug "adds 10
> years of lifespan".

- **Additive shifts:** Imply that a treatment adds a fixed quantity of time
  (e.g., "Drug A adds exactly 3 months of life").
- **Multiplicative shifts:** Imply that a treatment alters the rate at which
  time passes by a percentage or ratio (e.g., "Drug A extends survival duration
  by $40\%$").

Remember that the "Accelerated" in AFT refers to the effect of stressors or
relaxers on the baseline lifetime $T_0$ of a durable component:

$$T = T_0 \cdot \text{Combined Effect}$$

Taking the logarithm, we have the linear form:

$$ \ln T = \ln T_0 + \ln \left[\text{Combined Effect}\right] $$

so,

$$\ln \left[\text{Combined Effect}\right] = \boldsymbol{\beta}^\top \mathbf{x}$$

The term $\boldsymbol{\beta}^\top \mathbf{x}$ represents the log-scale
combined-effect of covariates.

### Weibull Distribution

> see
> {{< series-link "posts/analytics/general/distribution/weibull-distribution.en.md" "More on Weibull" >}}

The "Weibull" in "Weibull AFT" means that the random variable $T$ is assumed to
follow a Weibull distribution; therefore, the standardized error term $\epsilon$
follows a reference **minimum Gumbel distribution**.

> Remember: $\epsilon$ is on the log-time scale:
>
> **The logarithm of a Weibull RV gives a minimum Gumbel RV**.
>
> If
>
> $$Y \sim \operatorname{Weibull}(\text{scale } \lambda,\ \text{shape } k)$$
>
> then
>
> $$\ln Y \sim \operatorname{Gumbel}_{\min}\left(\text{location } \ln\lambda,\ \text{scale } \frac{1}{k}\right)$$
>
> In a reference $\operatorname{Gumbel}_{\min} (0, 1)$
>
> - location $\ln \lambda = 0, \; \lambda = 1$
> - scale $1/k = 1, \; k = 1$
>
> So, the reference distribution:
>
> $$\operatorname{Gumbel}_{\min} (\text{location} = 0,\text{scale} = 1) \iff \operatorname{Weibull}(\text{scale} = 1, \text{shape} = 1)$$

## Coefficient Interpretation: Time Ratio (TR)

AFT on Time scale:

$$T = \exp(\mu + \sigma \epsilon) \cdot \exp(\mathbf{x}^T \boldsymbol{\beta}) = T_0 \cdot \exp(\mathbf{x}^T \boldsymbol{\beta})$$

The multiplier $\exp(\mathbf{x}^T \boldsymbol{\beta})$ is known as the
**Acceleration Factor** or **Time Ratio (TR)**.

The marginal TR for covariate $j$:

$$\text{TR}_j = \exp(\beta_j)$$

- **$\beta_j > 0 \implies \text{TR}_j > 1$ (Time Extension):** (protective
  factor) The event is delayed.

  _Example:_ If a drug has $\beta = 0.405$, then
  $\text{TR} = \exp(0.405) \approx 1.50$. Treated subjects survive $1.5$ times
  as long ($50\%$ longer) as untreated subjects.

- **$\beta_j < 0 \implies \text{TR}_j < 1$ (Time Contraction):** (harmful
  factor) The event occurs earlier.

  _Example:_ If smoking has $\beta = -0.357$, then
  $\text{TR} = \exp(-0.357) \approx 0.70$. Smokers reach failure milestones in
  $70\%$ of the time it takes non-smokers.

## Model Diagnostics: Weibull Assumption

> **Assess whether the** Weibull **distribution** can effectively describe the
> process being modeled**.**

**Akaike Information Criterion (AIC):** Compare the fitted Weibull AFT model
against alternative parametric distributions (e.g., Log-Logistic, Log-Normal,
Generalized Gamma) to evaluate relative goodness of fit.

**Log-Cumulative Hazard Plot:** The curves should form straight lines
(confirming Weibull shape validity) and should run parallel to one another
(confirming the constant scale-shift assumption across covariate groups).

## More Distribution Assumptions for AFT

**Weibull / Exponential:** $\epsilon$ follows a standard minimum extreme value
distribution. Hazard can be monotonically increasing, decreasing, or constant.
Uniquely satisfies both AFT and Proportional Hazards assumptions simultaneously.

**Log-Logistic:** $\epsilon$ follows a standard logistic distribution. Yields a
unimodal (hump-shaped) hazard that increases initially, peaks, and then declines
(useful for modeling post-operative mortality or disease remission).

**Log-Normal:** $\epsilon$ follows a standard normal distribution. Similar to
Log-Logistic, exhibiting an initial increase followed by a long right-tail
decline, but with a heavier tail.

**Generalized Gamma:** A flexible 3-parameter family that encompasses the
Weibull, Log-Normal, and Exponential models as special cases, often used to test
which distribution best fits empirical data.
