## Kaplan-Meier(KM) Curves

Before fitting statistical models, we can establish an intuitive understanding
of the survival patterns characterized by each covariate. Just as we use scatter
plots to examine the dynamics between a response variable and an input
covariate, here we use a Kaplan-Meier (KM) curve instead of a scatter plot.

### Reading KM Curves

A KM chart tracks the estimated probability that a user remains "event-free"—in
this case, still subscribed—as their tenure unfolds.

As you scan KM curves across candidate covariates during exploratory data
analysis, focus on separation in the well-populated middle of the timeline and
treat erratic crossings in the sparse tail with skepticism.

Keep in mind, that KM curves are comparisons among categories from single
covariate. A covariate whose curves overlap on its own can still carry
predictive value in a multivariable model, treating KM plots a diagnostic
starting point rather than a feature-selection filter.

**Comparing Cohorts and Confidence Bands**

To assess whether a categorical covariate distinguishes retention outcomes,
examine the vertical separation between subgroup survival curves alongside their
**95% confidence intervals**.

Extensive overlap indicates that the covariate offers minimal discriminative
power for churn behavior. Conversely, when a covariate meaningfully influences
retention, its subgroup curves diverge early and maintain distinct separation
between their confidence bands.

**The At-Risk Table and the Sparse Tail**

A common pitfall in survival analysis is interpreting the right side of the plot
without consulting the **At risk** table beneath it, which tracks how many
subscribers remain active and under observation over time.

As the cohort shrinks toward the maximum tenure, statistical uncertainty grows.
Once fewer than `max(10, 5% of the initial group)` subjects remain under
observation, the plot flags the onset of the sparse tail with an `×` and
transitions the curve to a dashed line.

Below this threshold, even a single churn event can trigger a sharp drop in
estimated survival probability. Because this region—known as the **sparse
tail**—reflects small-sample noise rather than aggregate churn behavior,
late-stage survival estimates should be interpreted with caution.

lowest estimated survival, followed by one-year and then two-year contracts.

survival, while automatic bank-transfer and credit-card payments track closely
at higher levels. These patterns identify useful modeling candidates, but do not
establish that changing a contract or payment method would itself reduce churn.
