## What is a Kaplan-Meier (KM) Curve

The models discussed in later sections are all built with "covariates" in mind,
mapping an individual's profile $x$ to some representation of probability
measure $\mathcal{P}_{T^* \mid x}$, the survival function (CDF), for instance:

> A **covariate** is a measurable variable that can affect the outcome
> (dependent variable) in a statistical study, experiment, or regression model

$$x \longmapsto \hat{S}(t \mid x)$$

**Kaplan-Meier does not consider the covariates**. It observes the empirical
lifetime from a sample and attempts to build the empirical survival curve
directly:

$$\emptyset \longmapsto \hat{S}(t)$$

> A KM curve lets you inspect the observed **survival pattern** before fitting a
> survival regression.
>
> Like scatter plots. A scatter plot lets you inspect the observed relationship
> before fitting a regression.

## Estimate Survival using "Product-limit"

The KM calculation of survival is built on "product-limit":

$$\hat{S}(t) = \prod_{t_i \le t} \left(1 - \frac{d_i}{n_i}\right)$$

- $t_i$: Distinct ordered event times ($t_1 < t_2 < \dots < t_k$).
- $n_i$ (The Risk Set): Number of individuals known to be alive and uncensored
  just prior to $t_i$.
- $d_i$: Number of events occurring at time $t_i$.

> term $\frac{d_i}{n_i}$ means the "not-survived / failed" fraction out all
> remaining observable subjects at $t_i$, so $1 - \frac{d_i}{n_i}$ means the
> "survived" fraction.
>
> The probability of surviving $t_1$ is $1 - \frac{d_1}{n_1}$; the probability
> of surviving $t_2$ is the survivors from $t_1$ times the "surviving" fraction
> at $t_2$: $(1 - \frac{d_1}{n_1}) \times (1 - \frac{d_2}{n_2})$, therefore, the
> probability of surviving til $t_i$ is the product:
>
> $$\hat{S}(t) = \prod_{t_i \le t} \left(1 - \frac{d_i}{n_i}\right)$$

One specialty about KM counting is **how it counts the censored subjects**: an
individual censored at time $c$, for all event times $t_i \le c$, this subject
remains in the risk set $n_i$ (count as survivor). After time $c$, they drop out
of the risk set (do not count them at all, not as failure nor as survivor).

**Censored subjects are not completely ignored, and also neither treated as
immediate failures nor assumed to live forever; they provide information when
they still can and stop when they cannot**.

## $t$: The "Event-Driven" Clock

> **$t_i$ is not fixed periods**

The event times $t_1 < t_2 < \dots < t_k$ are **not fixed grid steps** (le.g.
hardcoded at 10, 20, 30).

- $t_1$ is **the timestamp of the first observed event in the entire study**.
- $t_2$ is **the timestamp of the second distinct event**, and so on.

The Kaplan–Meier Timeline is an Event-Driven Coordinate System:

- Aligned Origin: All subjects starts as $t=0$.
- **Irregular Coordinates**: The ticks ($t_1 < t_2 < \dots < t_k$) are the
  strictly ordered, each tick represents the lifetime elapse at which at least
  one event occurred.

  > To different subjects, the calendar time for $t = 3$ can be totally
  > different. $t=3$ may mean 2010/07/01 for Jim when he has stayed as
  > subscribers for 3 months, and 2020/08/01 for Marry when she has stayed as
  > subscribers also for 3 months

## Build a KM Curve

Suppose your analytics team pulls an observational snapshot from the CRM
database on **October 1, 2026**.

**The Transaction Log**

| Customer_ID | signup_date | churn_date | status   | Query Date |
| ----------- | ----------- | ---------- | -------- | ---------- |
| **User_A**  | 2025-11-01  | 2026-08-01 | Canceled | 2026-10-01 |
| **User_B**  | 2025-08-01  | NULL       | Active   | 2026-10-01 |
| **User_C**  | 2024-12-01  | 2026-09-01 | Canceled | 2026-10-01 |
| **User_D**  | 2024-04-01  | NULL       | Active   | 2026-10-01 |

**Event Timeline (Calendar Time)**

We transform calendar-time log to "tenure"-time state:

- (The Lifetime) **Tenure ($T$):** Elapsed subscription duration in months.
- (Event Indicator) **Churn ($\delta$):** $1$ if the user churned, $0$ if the
  account is still active at snapshot.

| Customer_ID | Tenure ($T$) | Churn ($\delta$) |
| ----------- | ------------ | ---------------- |
| User_A      | 9            | 1                |
| User_B      | 14           | 0                |
| User_C      | 21           | 1                |
| User_D      | 30           | 0                |

**Event Timeline (Lifetime/Tenure Time)**

Aligning each customer to relative tenure zero ($t_0 = 0$) reveals an irregular,
event-driven timeline where ticks occur at uneven intervals ($9$, $14$, $21$,
$30$) rather than periodic bins:

<figure style="text-align: center; width: 100%; max-width: 600px; margin: 30px auto;">
<img src="/img/analytics/kaplan-meier/km-event-timeline-tenure.png" alt="km-event-timeline-tenure" />
</figure>

With the above data, we can create **the aggregated Kaplan–Meier Life Table**

| $i$   | $t_i$ | $n_i$ | $d_i$ | Censored in $[t_i, t_{i+1})$ | Next Risk Set ($n_{i+1}$) | $1 - d_i / n_i$ | $\hat{S}(t_i)$     |
| ----- | ----- | ----- | ----- | ---------------------------- | ------------------------- | --------------- | ------------------ |
| $t_0$ | 0     | 4     | 0     | 0                            | 4                         | 1.000           | 1.0                |
| $t_1$ | 9     | 4     | 1 (A) | 1 (B @ M14)                  | 2 (Miss A, B)             | 0.75            | 1 x 0.75 = 0.75    |
| $t_2$ | 21    | 2     | 1 (C) | 1 (D @ M30)                  | 0 (Miss C, D)             | 0.5             | 0.5 x 0.75 = 0.375 |
